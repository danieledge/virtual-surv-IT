A virtual delivery team for transaction-monitoring, trade-surveillance and comms-surveillance engineering. Morgan (the PM) coordinates 16 named specialists to build, test, review and hand over solutions, keeping the evidence for each step as it goes.

How this actually works

The whole mental model, no framework jargon required. Dotted terms unfold: tap or hover for a plain-English definition.

§1

An AI framework: one orchestratororchestratorThe agent that plans and delegates rather than doing the work itself. Here, that is Morgan. Also called a lead or a router., multiple subagentsagent · subagentA separate AI worker with its own instructions, tools and memory. Morgan is one; each specialist is another.

Morgan orchestrates: planning, delegating. Mateo, Linh, Ravi and the rest are subagents, each with its own prompt, toolset and isolated contextcontextEverything the model can currently see: your messages, files it has read, results so far. It is finite, which is why each specialist gets a tight brief instead of the whole history., seeing only its brief. That isolation is a feature: because specialists cannot see one another's reasoning, every review stays independent.

§2

Morgan coordinates the work rather than performing it herself

Morgan is the PM. She takes your request, decides which specialists the job actually needs, briefs them precisely, checks what comes back, and reports to you. You deal with Morgan directly; the rest of the team works behind the scenes.

§3

A loop is an iteration cycle, not a failure

A failed check does not stop the work, it becomes another iteration until the work meets the standard. Same idea as a PR review: flagged, fixed, re-reviewed. The AI term is evaluator-optimizerevaluator-optimizerA two-role pattern: one pass produces the work, a separate pass judges it against a standard, and the work loops until it clears. The judging pass is deliberately not the author.: one pass produces the work, an independent pass checks it against a named standard, and it ships once that pass clears.

§4

Talk to Morgan like you would a real PM

There is no command language to learn. Describe the outcome you want, in your own words. Morgan asks clarifying questions, flags anything unrealistic or risky, and checks in before anything material happens. Hand her context exactly as you would a colleague (a screenshot, a spec, an existing script) and she will work from it.

§5

📊 and 🧠 separate what was checked from what was reasoned

These are the tags you will see in every report, so they are worth knowing. 📊 marks something verified directly: a test run, a query executed, a metric taken from the data. 🧠 marks reasoning that goes beyond the evidence, still useful, but flagged so it is not read as fact, which is the practical guard against hallucinationhallucinationConfident output that is not grounded in anything real. Tagging every claim by its basis makes the difference visible instead of leaving you to guess.. 📄 marks a value read straight from source code, with nothing run.

§6

Every piece of work gets its own folder

Each engagement lives in artifacts/<name>/: its brief, specs, code, tests, and a living START-HERE.md status page showing where it stands, written to disk precisely so it survives compactioncompactionWhat happens when a long conversation is summarised to fit. Anything that matters is kept on disk so it is not lost when this happens.. Several can sit in the same project at once, each independent; one root index lists them all.

Getting started

The team is dormant by default. Nothing below happens until you ask for it.

  1. /engage

    Type this to start anything. It is the one command to remember.

  2. Describe the work in plain English: a problem, a review, or something to build.
    e.g. "Build a wash-trading detection scenario for the equities desk."

    No need to name a specialist; Morgan routes it.

  3. Answer the menus Morgan opens (depth, scope, outcome). Pick an option, or just tell Morgan what is needed.
  4. Watch it work. Morgan briefs the specialists and reports back at each gate.

What people ask first

The questions that come up before anyone trusts a team of agents with real work.

Will it change my code without asking?

Builders write files; the reviewers and specialists who advise cannot edit anything, by tool grant rather than by request. Work lands in the engagement's own folder unless you ask for it in your source tree, nothing is committed or pushed for you, and each phase stops at a gate for your answer.

What does one engagement cost, and how long?

Across the 12 engagements in our own most recent test run: $0.53 to $16.82 (median about $4) and 3 to 42 minutes (median 13). Those were headless runs that cannot reuse the specialists' prompt cache, so an interactive session should cost less. Bigger scope means more specialists, and more specialists means more tokens.

Where does my data go?

Anything you point the team at is sent to the model provider as context, the same as any other Claude session. The protected raw-data folder is blocked outright; everything else is your judgement call, which is why the sheet keeps saying to mask or synthesise first.

Why a team, rather than just asking Claude?

One assistant checking its own output is marking its own homework. Here the reviewer never saw the builder's reasoning, so the second opinion is genuinely independent. That separation is the entire point, and it is also why this costs more than a single prompt.

Will I get the same answer twice?

Not word for word. The mechanical checks are deterministic: same input, same result, every time. The judgement parts vary at the margin, so wording, ordering and genuinely borderline findings can shift between runs, while a clear-cut one is far more stable. Treat any single run as one careful pass rather than an exhaustive sweep: reports record the model and version, and absence of a finding is not evidence of absence. If something is load-bearing, verify it rather than relying on one pass.

Can I stop it half way?

Yes, interrupt whenever you like. Anything already written stays on disk and the engagement records itself as blocked rather than finished, so it never quietly looks complete. A later session reads that state and picks up where it stopped.

Best practices

  • Mention your materials: a screenshot, a spec, an existing script. Morgan works from what you hand her, and guesses less when you hand her more.
  • Say what "done" means: review only, or also fixed, or also handed over. Agreed before work starts beats renegotiated after.
  • Push back. Morgan is meant to be a sceptic rather than a yes-man, and so are you.   • Do not hand it unmasked data. Use /prepare-data, or better, go synthetic. Pseudonymised is not anonymous.
